A guide for older teenagers

Being “digital” is not the same as being digitally safe

Most teenagers are confident users of phones, platforms and apps. Confidence, however, is not the same as security, privacy, critical judgement or media literacy. AI adds a new layer: content can now be produced at enormous speed, conversations can feel convincingly human, and images, voices and documents can be fabricated with increasing realism.

The aim is not to fear technology. The aim is to use it deliberately: protect your accounts and data, recognise manipulation, verify important claims and keep human judgement in control.
01 • The digital safety foundation

Seven principles of safe online behaviour

1 • Security
Protect access to your accounts.

Use passkeys where available, or strong unique passwords with two-step verification. Keep devices and apps updated.

2 • Privacy
Share less than you think you need to.

Personal details, locations, school information, documents and private photos can be copied, combined or used for impersonation.

3 • Consent
Other people’s data is not yours to upload.

Ask before posting, forwarding or submitting someone else’s photo, voice, message or work to an AI system.

4 • Verification
Fluent does not mean factual.

Check important information against reliable, independent sources — especially when an AI system supplies facts, quotations, citations or statistics.

5 • Reputation
Your digital footprint has an audience you cannot fully predict.

Assume that screenshots and copies may survive after a post is deleted.

6 • Financial caution
Urgency is a warning sign.

Slow down when a message pressures you to pay, invest, transfer money, reveal a code or “act now”.

7 • Human control
Use AI as a tool, not an authority.

You remain responsible for decisions, submissions, messages and consequences — even when AI suggested them.

Golden rule
Pause before you click, post, pay or prompt.

A ten-second pause can prevent a permanent mistake.

Strong account hygiene: unique passwords or passkeys, two-step verification, automatic updates, screen lock, backups, and immediate action when an account behaves strangely.
02 • Artificial intelligence changes the risk landscape

AI literacy: useful, powerful — not automatically trustworthy

Generative AI can create text, images, audio, video and code. It can support learning and creativity, but it may also invent information, reproduce bias, expose data, automate scams or make manipulation more convincing.

Hallucination

An AI system may produce a confident answer that is inaccurate or entirely fabricated.

Synthetic media

Images, audio and video may be AI-generated or altered. Realistic appearance is no longer proof of authenticity.

Data exposure

Prompts may contain personal, confidential or copyrighted material. Treat anything you upload as information you may lose control over.

Bias

AI systems can reflect patterns, stereotypes and gaps found in training data and system design.

Persuasive fluency

AI can sound authoritative even when evidence is weak. Style is not a substitute for truth.

Automation at scale

AI can help criminals generate personalised phishing, fake reviews, cloned voices and convincing impersonation.

Never paste into a general AI chatbot: passwords, authentication codes, bank details, identity documents, private school records, intimate images, confidential workplace information, or another person’s private data without permission.

Think Before You Trust: the S.E.E.D. check

S — Source
Who is behind the claim? Is there an identifiable original source?
E — Evidence
What evidence supports it? Can you find independent confirmation?
E — Environment
What is the date, context, platform and purpose? Could an algorithm be amplifying it?
D — Digital manipulation
Could the image, voice, quotation or document have been generated or altered?
03 • Education

Use AI to strengthen learning — not replace it

Learning with digital tools. Photo: Mikhail Seleznev / Unsplash.

Productive uses

  • Ask for an explanation at a different difficulty level.
  • Generate practice questions or vocabulary exercises.
  • Brainstorm possible approaches before writing.
  • Request feedback on clarity, organisation or grammar.
  • Compare several viewpoints and then verify them.
  • Use AI for role-play, interview practice or technical-English simulations.

Risky or dishonest uses

  • Submitting AI-generated work as if you wrote it yourself.
  • Copying citations without opening and checking the source.
  • Uploading confidential school material or classmates’ work.
  • Letting AI make a judgement you are expected to make.
  • Using AI output when your teacher or assessment rules prohibit it.
  • Assuming an “AI detector” can prove authorship with certainty.
A good academic habit: keep a simple AI-use record: tool used, purpose, important prompts, what you accepted or rejected, and which claims you independently verified. Follow your school’s disclosure rules.

UNESCO’s guidance on generative AI in education emphasises a human-centred approach, data privacy, age-appropriate use and meaningful human agency rather than replacing learners’ judgement.

04 • Social life

Friends, followers, chatbots and digital identity

Phones are social spaces as well as personal devices. Photo: Vitaly Gariev / Unsplash.
Privacy is social.

A group photo, voice note or screenshot can reveal information about several people. Consent matters even when you are “only” sharing with friends.

Private accounts are not private rooms.

Followers can screenshot, record or forward content. Use privacy settings, but do not treat them as a guarantee.

AI companions are systems, not confidential friends.

Do not assume a chatbot relationship is private, neutral or emotionally equivalent to a human relationship.

Deepfakes can harm real people.

Creating or sharing deceptive or non-consensual synthetic images of someone can cause severe harm and may have legal consequences.

Cyberbullying travels.

Humiliation can be multiplied by reposts, group chats and recommendation systems. Do not join pile-ons because “everyone else is doing it”.

Unknown contact deserves caution.

Grooming, impersonation and manipulation often begin with apparently friendly conversation and gradual requests for secrecy or private material.

If someone pressures you for intimate images, money, secrecy or continued contact: stop responding, save evidence, block/report the account and speak to a trusted adult or appropriate authority. Do not pay an extortion demand in the hope that the problem will disappear.
05 • Economy & money

AI-era scams, shopping and financial decisions

Online shopping can combine convenience with financial risk. Photo: Vitaly Gariev / Unsplash.
Voice-clone emergency scams

A caller may sound like a relative or friend. Do not trust the voice alone. End the call and verify using a number or channel you already know.

Phishing gets more convincing

AI can produce polished emails and messages. Check the sender, destination URL and request — not just spelling and grammar.

Fake endorsements

A video of a celebrity or influencer apparently promoting an investment or product may be synthetic or manipulated.

“Free” may still cost you

Trials, subscriptions, in-app purchases and data collection can create costs that are not obvious at the start.

Investment hype

Claims of guaranteed returns, secret algorithms or urgent cryptocurrency opportunities are major warning signs.

Payment methods matter

Requests for gift cards, cryptocurrency or unusual transfers are common scam indicators because money can be difficult to recover.

Money safety routine: pause → verify independently → ask someone you trust → check the merchant or organisation directly → pay only through a legitimate channel → keep transaction records and alerts enabled.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission warns that scammers can use short online audio clips to clone a person’s voice for fake family-emergency schemes. Its advice is to verify the story through a known contact method rather than trusting the voice.

06 • News & information

When seeing is no longer believing

Disinformation can be produced at scale

AI lowers the cost of producing convincing text, images and audio. A false story can look professionally written and visually credible.

Photo: Insaanu Studio / Unsplash.

Your feed is selected, not neutral

Recommendation systems prioritise content according to signals and platform objectives. Popularity and repetition are not evidence of accuracy.

Photo: Rich Smith / Unsplash.

The 5-question verification routine

Who published it?
Find the original publisher, author or organisation — not just the account that reposted it.
What is the evidence?
Look for documents, direct quotations, data, named witnesses or traceable sources.
What do independent sources say?
Check whether reliable outlets or primary sources confirm the claim.
Is the context complete?
An old image, cropped video or true statement placed in a false context can mislead.
Could it be synthetic?
Look for provenance and original uploads. Do not rely on a single “AI detector” as definitive proof.
Three useful distinctions: misinformation is false or inaccurate information shared without necessarily intending harm; disinformation is deliberately deceptive; malinformation uses genuine information in a harmful or manipulative way, often by stripping it of context or violating privacy.
07 • Response plan

If something goes wrong online

Do not panic or rush.
Urgency benefits scammers and manipulators.
Stop the interaction.
Do not keep arguing, paying or sending more information.
Preserve evidence.
Save relevant screenshots, usernames, URLs, dates and transaction details where appropriate.
Secure the account.
Change compromised credentials, end unknown sessions and enable stronger authentication.
Block and report.
Use the platform’s reporting tools for impersonation, harassment, harmful content or fraud.
Tell a trusted person.
For teenagers, involving a trusted adult, teacher or safeguarding professional can prevent escalation.
Escalate serious cases.
Threats, extortion, financial theft, stalking or sexual exploitation may require the platform, bank, school safeguarding team, helpline or police.
08 • Classroom challenge

What would you do?

Scenario 1 — AI homework shortcut

You ask an AI chatbot to write your assignment. It produces a polished answer with three references.

Your task: Identify at least four things you must do before using any part of the response.

Suggested answer: Check school rules; verify the references exist; read the sources yourself; rewrite from your own understanding; disclose AI assistance if required; remove personal/confidential data from prompts.

Scenario 2 — “It sounds exactly like my brother”

You receive a call from someone who sounds like your brother. He says his phone is damaged and urgently needs money transferred to a new account.

Best response: End the call and contact your brother through a known number or another trusted person. Do not trust voice identity alone.

Scenario 3 — Viral breaking-news video

A dramatic video is being shared by many accounts, but none links to the original source.

Best response: Pause sharing; find the earliest/original upload; check credible independent reporting; verify date and location; consider synthetic or edited media.

Scenario 4 — AI “friend” asks personal questions

A chatbot conversation becomes very personal and asks about your location, family problems and private photos.

Best response: Do not treat the chatbot as confidential. Avoid sharing sensitive information; review the service’s privacy settings and stop the conversation if it becomes uncomfortable or inappropriate.

Scenario 5 — A friend becomes a meme

Someone uses AI to alter a classmate’s photo and posts it as a joke. Other students start reposting it.

Best response: Do not amplify it. Consider consent, humiliation and potential harm; support the person targeted and use appropriate reporting/safeguarding channels.

Useful advanced phrases for discussion

“What evidence supports that claim?”
“Can we trace this back to the original source?”
“The content is plausible, but it has not been verified.”
“This may be authentic, manipulated or entirely synthetic.”
“I used AI for feedback, not to replace my own work.”
“I would not share that information without consent.”
“The message creates urgency, which is a common manipulation tactic.”
“A realistic presentation is not evidence of factual accuracy.”
Official guidance & further reading

Reliable starting points

  • UNESCO — Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research: human-centred, privacy-aware and age-appropriate AI use.
  • UNICEF — Digital Education: Safety: online learning opportunities and risks for children and young people.
  • UNICEF — Keeping Children Safe Online: AI, exploitation, cyberbullying, harmful content and manipulative digital practices.
  • European Commission — DSA Guidelines on the Protection of Minors: privacy, recommender systems, blocking/muting and safer platform design.
  • FTC Consumer Advice — Fake Emergency Scams: including AI voice-cloning risks.
  • UK National Cyber Security Centre — Password Managers & Secure Accounts: unique credentials, password managers, passkeys and two-step verification.
